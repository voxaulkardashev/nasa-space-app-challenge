# Holuhraun Lava Field, Iceland: team research draft (now on the website)

This is the team's original draft. The checked, edited version is on the website (`src/data/holuhraunResearch.ts`); what was confirmed or left uncited is listed in `docs/SOURCES.md`.

## Introduction
Holuhraun is a large basaltic lavafield in the Icelandic highlands within the Bárðarbunga–Veiðivötn volcanic system. It is located on the Dyngjusandur floodplain, north of the Vatnajökull ice cap and south of the Askja volcanic system.

There was a major fissure-fed volcanic eruption in this area between August 2014 and February 2015 that covered an area of around 84 square kilometers with basaltic lava. This was the largest effusive volcanic eruption in Iceland since 1784, making it a great opportunity to study the geological influence such volcanic activity can have on the terrain and how it may contribute to creating warm and water-rich environments that support microbial life.

This eruption allowed for the interaction between newly erupted lava and the surrounding glacial river system. The lava intersected waterways that were supplied by glacial meltwater as it spread across the floodplain. This resulted in the formation of temporary hydrothermal environments, making the area a good way to study how volcanic heat, liquid water, and basaltic rock can combine to create conditions capable of hosting life. Many volcanic landscapes are studied and compared to Mars largely because of their geological features, but Holuhraun is unique because it can also be investigated for its microbial communities and usefulness in astrobiological research.

## Earth Analog Reasoning
We believe that Holuhraun is a viable terrestrial analogue for an aspect of Elysium Planitia other than its volcanic activity. We were intrigued by the potential formation of habitable hydrothermal environments through interactions between basaltic lava and water on the Martian plains, and we believe finding analogous conditions on Earth will help scientists investigate this intriguing astrobiological possibility.

During the 2014–2015 eruption, lava entered a glacial river system and heated the surrounding water, creating temporary hydrothermal pools and springs. This in turn created an environment in which volcanic heat, liquid water, and basaltic minerals all interacted, providing conditions that supported microbial life. Similar processes may have taken place in Elysium Planitia when volcanic lava interacted with subsurface water and ice. Holuhraun does not reproduce the Martian atmosphere accurately and also does not account for the effects of temperature and subsurface conditions, but it does offer a scientifically valuable environment where we can investigate how volcanic activity can generate temporary habitable niches on this planet and others.

Previous studies have already studied the formation of living microbial communities in this area while the lava-heated water was warm (up to 48 degrees C). But on Mars, scientists would be searching for incredibly ancient, fossilized biosignatures from billions of years ago. Therefore, we think a suitable approach would be to focus on what happens after the hydrothermal system dies, studying how rapid cooling and mineral precipitation in the now-cold Holuhraun outflow channels entrap or degrade the original molecules and biomarkers left behind by those temporary proliferations of thermophilic bacteria. This would answer a very important question capable of informing future Mars research; are short lava-heated hydrothermal systems on Mars enough to leave detectable biosignatures?

## Mineral Composition
Holuhraun is mostly composed of tholeiitic basalt, which is a common type of volcanic rock that contains iron and magnesium. Some other minerals identified in studies of the 2014–2015 eruption were plagioclase feldspar (containing calcium and sodium), clinopyroxene, olivine, and titanomagnetite. The groundmass contained microlites of plagioclase, clinopyroxene, olivine, and skeletal titanomagnetite. The lava also contained cavities created by escaping volcanic gases during cooling and solidification. Those cavities and fractures are possibly important for investigating how fluids move through the volcanic rock and where microbial communities might be able to establish colonies.

The basaltic composition of this area makes it very useful for drawing comparisons between water-rock interactions on Earth and in Elysium Planitia. Specifically the alteration of volcanic materials by heated water could have been a catalyst that released dissolved elements and created chemically diverse environments capable of supporting life.

## Terrain and Landforms
Holuhraun has a broad, extensive, generally flat basaltic lava plain that was formed through fissure-fed volcanic activity. The 2014–2015 eruption produced rubbly, slabby, and spiny pāhoehoe (meaning lava with fragmented, plate-like, and spiny surface structures) and also areas of rough and jagged lava composed of broken and angular fragments. These formations altogether created a landscape that contains fragmented crust, vesicles, fractures, and irregular surfaces. The lava field formed over the glacial outwash plain which already had sand, gravel, and river channels that facilitated interactions between lava and water.

Interesting to note here that this kind of environment could provide an opportunity for scientists to investigate whether old sediment/terrain artifacts and biosignatures can survive underneath younger lava episodes.

## Current Life & Biosphere
The 2015 explosion produced a temporary hydrothermal system that was capable of hosting confirmed microbial colonies. When the freshly erupted lava began interacting with the glacial meltwater, volcanic heat created warmer aquatic environments than before (hot pools, springs, hydrothermal waters). These lava-heated water environments have been found to contain microbes.

A study also investigated microbial communities associated with Holuhraun hydrothermal systems in 2016 and found that the microbial communities in the lava-heated water were substantially different from those found in the original glacier river and spring water. Some of the microbial groups were actually exclusive to the lava-heated water. Also, microbial abundance, richness, and diversity increased overall in experimental warm-water environments compared with cooler controls. Researchers isolated thermophilic bacteria from the lava-heated water and a nearby acidic hydrothermal system.

However, intensive biological monitoring of this environment declined after the hydrothermal oases caused by the 2014–2015 eruption cooled. We propose long-term post-cooling monitoring of these previously heated lava-water sites. By tracking how microbial communities, organic molecules, and biosignatures evolve, entomb, or decay after hydrothermal heating has stopped, we can determine whether short-lived volcanically heated conditions on ancient Mars could have preserved detectable biosignatures billions of years after freezing.

## Climate/Temperature/Weather
The lava-induced hydrothermal system increased the area's local water temperatures from around 6 °C to 48.4 °C (6 months post eruption) before dropping roughly back to normal roughly two years post eruption. This occurred because the cold glacial meltwater and springwater percolated through porous and fractured basalt during the lava eruption and formed warm springs, pools, and seepage channels.

## Elevation
The Holuhraun lava fields have an elevation of approximately 800 meters above sea level. This ranges due to the uneven nature of the eruptions, erosion, and deposition.
